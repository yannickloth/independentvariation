Workflow Patterns as Knowledge-Gap Indicators

A list of short posts that read each pattern as a completeness check on domain knowledge.

Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Case study: Deferred Choice

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

van der Aalst et al.: A point where the choice among alternatives is deferred until an event enables one of them. [1]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

The fact that the world picks the branch, at the moment it happens, is domain knowledge. If the business says “either the customer pays or the order expires — whichever comes first,” the choice is not made by the process but by an external event that commits one path and withdraws the others. Deferred Choice is that fact made executable: a branch point where the first event to occur commits one path and withdraws the rest.

ImportantWhen the model is faithful

The domain genuinely cannot pre-decide: which alternative occurs is settled by the environment, not by a condition the process evaluates. A deferred choice models this honestly — the alternatives are live until one event commits, and the rest are withdrawn. The engine waits for the world.

WarningWhen the model misrepresents

The engine’s exclusive choice is used instead, with a condition evaluated at the gateway. Then the model asserts “we decide here” when the domain says “the customer does.” The process picks a winner the business never picked, and cases take paths that no real event selected. The notation silently downgraded a world-made decision into an engine-made one.

TipThe faithfulness test

Is the branch chosen by us at a known point, or by something outside the process at an unknown time?

  • If the world chooses → deferred choice is the domain’s own semantics.
  • If we choose by rule → exclusive choice is faithful.
  • If you can’t answer → you’ve found a knowledge gap: the business hasn’t said who decides.

Case study: Milestone

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

van der Aalst et al.: A milestone enables a task only while a named state holds. [1]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

The fact that a step is permitted only while a named state still holds is domain knowledge. “An order may be changed only before it ships”; “a claim may be reopened only within thirty days.” A Milestone is that fact made executable: a condition on a state, not a sequence position — an activity enabled because a named situation currently holds.

ImportantWhen the model is faithful

The business genuinely gates work on a state, not on reaching a step: the state may hold, lapse, or be restored by other paths. Modelling the milestone makes the gate explicit and correct — the activity is permitted exactly while the condition is true, wherever the process happens to be.

WarningWhen the model misrepresents

The gate is modelled as an ordering — “this task comes after that task” — so the rule “only before shipping” becomes “only in this branch.” Then re-entry, rollback, and concurrent paths bypass a rule the business meant absolutely. The model asserts a sequence where the domain stated a state.

TipThe faithfulness test

Would an expert state this as a condition (“only while…”) or as an order (“only after…”)?

  • If condition → milestone; the state is the domain’s own gate.
  • If order → sequence is faithful.
  • If you can’t answer → the knowledge gap is the gate itself: nobody has said when the step is allowed.

Case study: Cancellation Region

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

van der Aalst et al.: An activity that cancels all activities running inside its region, including itself. [1]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

The fact that the business may abandon work already in flight — and that abandonment withdraws everything downstream, not just one step — is domain knowledge. “An order may be cancelled, and everything queued for it stops”; “a claim may be withdrawn, and all its pending checks end.” A Cancellation Region is that fact made executable: a scope that, once cancelled, retires every activity inside it.

ImportantWhen the model is faithful

The domain recognises withdrawal as a first-class act with a real scope: work is not merely skipped but ended, and no later step proceeds on a dead case. Modelling the region makes the scope of abandonment explicit and matches how the business talks about it.

WarningWhen the model misrepresents

Cancellation is modelled as a branch that “goes somewhere else,” leaving the other threads running. Then the process continues work on a case the domain considers cancelled: invoices for withdrawn orders, checks for abandoned claims. The model asserts the case is alive when the business ended it.

TipThe faithfulness test

Does abandoning this work end everything downstream, across all paths — or is it just a different route?

  • If it ends the work → cancellation region; the scope is the domain’s own.
  • If it reroutes → a branch is faithful.
  • If you can’t answer → the gap is the scope of abandonment, which nobody has stated.

Case study: Exclusive Choice vs. Multi-Choice

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

van der Aalst et al.: An exclusive choice (XOR-split) enables exactly one branch; a multi-choice (OR-split) may enable several. [1]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

How many branches may run at a split is a domain claim. Exclusive choice asserts “exactly one applies”; multi-choice asserts “any subset may apply, including several.” If the business says “every applicable discount is applied” or “each defect class present triggers its own inspection,” the multiplicity is knowledge — and the gateway choice records it.

ImportantWhen the model is faithful

The business distinguishes “one of these” from “some of these”: a claim is either accepted or denied (exclusive), but an order may attract several promotions (multi-choice). The gateway matches the domain’s own “how many may apply” rule, and the model’s behaviour on a multi-applicable case is what the business expects.

WarningWhen the model misrepresents

Multi-choice is silently modelled as exclusive, or vice versa. Then a case that should trigger three inspections triggers one, or a case that admits exactly one decision takes two. The failure is quiet: the process runs, the count is simply wrong. The notation asserted a multiplicity the domain never stated.

TipThe faithfulness test

Can several branches apply at once, or exactly one?

  • If several → multi-choice; the subset rule is the domain’s own.
  • If exactly one → exclusive choice.
  • If you can’t answer → the gap is multiplicity: nobody has said whether these overlap.

Case study: Parallel Split & Synchronization

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

van der Aalst et al.: A parallel split (AND-split) enables all branches; a synchronization (AND-join) waits for all of them. [1]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

Two facts are claimed here: the sub-works run at the same time independently, and the next step is meaningful only once all of them have finished. “A loan is checked for credit, fraud, and compliance concurrently; the decision waits for all three.” Parallel Split asserts co-occurrence; Synchronization asserts a barrier. Both are domain claims — the same concurrency questions as the Barrier pattern, now stated as claims about the process.

ImportantWhen the model is faithful

The domain genuinely has independent sub-works with no order between them, and a convergence point that requires every one. The model’s concurrency and its wait-for-all both match how the business speaks: three checks, then a decision.

WarningWhen the model misrepresents

The sub-works are actually ordered or dependent, and modelling them as parallel hides a constraint the business relies on; or the convergence does not need everyone, and synchronization blocks cases that the domain would pass. Worse, a missing synchronization (two paths, no join) lets a case proceed on partial results and never notice — the join’s absence is invisible in the picture.

TipThe faithfulness test

Are these sub-works genuinely independent and jointly required — and does the next step truly wait for all of them?

  • If yes → parallel split + synchronization is faithful.
  • If no → the ordering or the wait is wrong; the model asserts concurrency or a barrier the domain doesn’t have.
  • If you can’t answer → the gap is the dependency graph: nobody has settled what must complete before what.

Case study: Gateways & Token Semantics

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

OMG: BPMN gateways control how a token is consumed and produced to route a process. [2]

What the token is — and is not

A token looks like pure engine bookkeeping — “how far has this case advanced.” But the split and join rules built on tokens are still domain decisions, not engine detail: each join asserts “any one arrival is enough,” “all are required,” or “some are.” The engine’s default is not neutral; it picks one of those rules, and the domain must authorise it.

ImportantWhen the join semantics are faithful

An XOR join passes each arrival through — it asserts “arrival via any one path completes this convergence; the paths are genuine alternatives.” When the business says “approval OR rejection finishes the review,” that is exactly right, and the weakest convergence is the correct one.

WarningWhen the join semantics misrepresent — the default trap

The engine defaults to the XOR join, and nobody asks the “any vs. all vs. some” question. If the business actually requires all paths (both the credit check and the fraud check before the decision), the model proceeds on money it never received. A deadlocking token model, likewise, exposes that the domain’s flow is not a BPMN-shaped graph at all: the notation found a gap for you.

TipThe faithfulness test

For every convergence: does the next step need any one arrival, all of them, or some?

  • If any → XOR join.
  • If all → parallel/immediate synchronization.
  • If some → inclusive join.
  • If you can’t answer → you’ve found the gap: the weak default is standing in for a question nobody asked.

Case study: Swimlanes

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

OMG: Swimlanes partition a process by the participant, role, or system that performs each activity. [2]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

The fact that a step belongs to a particular actor is domain knowledge — and it is authority, not convenience. “Only the underwriter approves the policy”; “the warehouse, not sales, confirms dispatch.” Swimlanes are that fact made executable: the lane claims “this actor owns this step,” and crossing a lane claims a hand-off between parties.

ImportantWhen the lanes are faithful

The domain assigns responsibility: a role, a department, an external party owns each activity, and the hand-offs between lanes are real business transfers (“we send it to the insurer and wait”). The lanes match how the business describes ownership and accountability, and the model’s boundaries are the domain’s own.

WarningWhen the lanes misrepresent

Lanes are drawn for diagram tidiness — “this looks like a different system” — so the picture asserts ownership nobody has. Then the model claims a hand-off where none occurs, and readers infer authority from layout. The swimlane is the most-read element of a BPMN diagram, which makes a false lane the most consequential lie on the page.

TipThe faithfulness test

Would an expert name the owner of this step as a distinct party — and describe passing work between lanes as a real hand-off?

  • If yes → the lane is the domain’s own authority boundary.
  • If no → the lane is layout; it asserts ownership the business never assigned.
  • If you can’t answer → the gap is responsibility: nobody has said who owns the step.

Case study: Boundary Events

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

OMG: A boundary event attaches to an activity to model an interrupting or non-interrupting event. [2]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

The fact that the outside world can interrupt a step already running is domain knowledge. “The patient deteriorates during the procedure”; “the customer cancels while the order is being picked”; “the deadline passes while the claim waits.” A Boundary Event is that fact made executable: an interruption attached to an activity’s lifetime, available only while it runs.

ImportantWhen the boundaries are faithful

The domain recognises real interruptions with defined responses: a timeout, an escalation, a cancellation with cleanup. Attaching the event to the activity’s lifetime matches the domain’s own “this can be interrupted, and here is what we do” rule — and the model ends or reroutes the step exactly as the business would.

WarningWhen the boundaries misrepresent

Interruptions are modelled as later steps in the sequence (“after the procedure, check whether the patient deteriorated”) — which claims the interruption is knowable only afterwards, and cannot stop the running work. Or a non-interrupting event is used where the domain needs the activity stopped. The model asserts control the business does not have.

TipThe faithfulness test

Can the real world end or change this step while it is happening — with a response the business has defined?

  • If yes → a boundary event is the domain’s own rule; attach it to the lifetime.
  • If no → sequence is faithful; a boundary invents an interruption.
  • If you can’t answer → the gap is the interruption rule — and it is usually discovered in production.

Case study: Case Management / Ad-hoc Processes

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

OMG: Case management models knowledge-driven work whose steps are not known in advance. [3]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

The fact that the order of steps is not knowable in advance is domain knowledge. In investigations, clinical care, and legal matters, a knowledge worker decides the next act as the case unfolds — guided by goals and constraints, not by a fixed route. Case Management is that fact made executable: a case file with milestones and available actions, rather than a prescribed sequence.

ImportantWhen the model is faithful

The domain genuinely requires judgement about what comes next: the situation determines the step, and experts would resist a fixed flow. A case model keeps the goal, the constraints, and the history, and lets the worker choose. The absence of a fixed route is the domain’s own truth, not a gap in the model.

WarningWhen the model misrepresents

A rigid workflow is imposed where the order is genuinely emergent — so real work goes around the system, and the recorded process diverges from the performed one. Or the reverse: a case model is used where the domain actually mandates a fixed sequence (regulatory steps, safety checks), and the model permits omissions the business forbids. Both lies are about the same question: is the route known?

TipThe faithfulness test

Would experts say the next step depends on judgement as the case unfolds — or that it is prescribed?

  • If judgement → case management is faithful; the goal and constraints are the domain’s own.
  • If prescribed → a fixed flow is faithful; a case model permits what the domain forbids.
  • If you can’t answer → the gap is exactly the degree of prescription nobody has settled.

Case study: Process Mining / Conformance Checking

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

van der Aalst: Process mining derives process models from event logs and checks observed behaviour against a model. [4]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

This one measures the gap directly. The believed process (the model) and the actual process (the event log) are two claims about the same domain, and conformance checking is the difference between them. Where the log deviates from the model, one of two things is true: the model is wrong, or the workarounds are wrong — and someone must decide which.

ImportantWhen the model and log agree

The recorded steps match the modelled flow, and the business confirms the model reflects how work is really performed. The model is then genuine knowledge, and it can be trusted to reason about change.

WarningWhen they diverge — the strongest indicator in the family

The log shows paths the model forbids, steps the model never mentions, and rework the picture hides. Every deviation is a place where the real business knowledge escaped the model — often a rule that was never articulated, an exception handled by habit. Mining is the only indicator here that cannot be argued with: it reports what happened, not what was believed.

TipThe faithfulness test

Does the log match the model — and if it does not, which one does the business intend to be true?

  • If they match → the model is knowledge.
  • If they diverge → the deviation is the gap; resolve it with the experts, in one direction or the other.
  • If you can’t answer → you have found the gap without even needing a question.

Case study: Choreography vs. Orchestration

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

Peltz: Orchestration centralizes control in a single coordinator; choreography distributes it across cooperating peers. [5]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

Who sees and who coordinates the process is organizational knowledge. Orchestration puts one party in charge of the whole flow; choreography describes each participant’s behaviour and lets no one own the whole. “We drive the process with our suppliers” versus “each party acts on the messages the others send” is a real, and often disputed, business distinction.

ImportantWhen the model is faithful

The domain has a genuine coordinator — a platform, a lead party, a clearing house — and orchestration records that authority accurately. Or the domain is genuinely peer-to-peer, and the choreography correctly denies any one party full visibility. Either way, the model’s visibility boundary is one the business recognises.

WarningWhen the model misrepresents

Orchestration is chosen because the tool makes it easy, and the model asserts a central authority the business never granted — the coordinator now holds knowledge that was never theirs to hold. The reverse is just as common: a choreography is drawn over what is in fact one party driving everything, hiding the dependency and the accountability. The model claims a distribution of control that does not exist.

TipThe faithfulness test

Does one party genuinely have the whole picture and drive the flow, or does each participant know only its own part?

  • If one driver → orchestration is the domain’s own authority.
  • If peers → choreography.
  • If you can’t answer → the gap is ownership of the process, which nobody has assigned.

Case study: State Machines for Business Entities

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

Harel: Statecharts model the permitted states and transitions of a system. [6]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

The fact that a thing may change only in ways the business allows is domain knowledge. “An order cannot go from shipped back to draft”; “a claim cannot be approved before it is assessed.” A state machine is that fact made executable: named states and permitted transitions, so the entity’s allowed changes are stated once and enforced everywhere.

ImportantWhen the model is faithful

The business names the states and the moves between them, and experts recognise a forbidden transition as a real error (“you can’t un-ship an order”). The state machine is the domain’s own rule about how things may change, and every code path that touches the entity obeys it.

WarningWhen the model misrepresents

A status field is written freely by whichever code path runs, and the “states” are whatever strings appear. Then the model claims transitions the business forbids — a refunded invoice that is later marked paid, an approved claim that was never assessed. The absence of a state machine is not neutral: it asserts that any change is possible, which is a claim the domain denies.

TipThe faithfulness test

Can an expert list the states and say which moves are forbidden — and would a forbidden move be a business error?

  • If yes → the state machine is the domain’s own transition rule.
  • If no → free-form status is faithful; a machine would invent states.
  • If you can’t answer → the gap is the lifecycle: nobody has said what this thing’s allowed changes are.

Case study: EPC — Event/Function Alternation

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

Scheer: An event-driven process chain alternates events and functions to describe a process. [7]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

EPC’s structural claim is that events and functions alternate: every action is triggered by a state, and produces one. That is a domain distinction — an event is a fact the business observes (“what happened”), a function is an act the business chose (“what we did”). These are different kinds, and EPC forces the difference where BPMN does not.

ImportantWhen the model is faithful

The business genuinely distinguishes the state that licenses an action from the action itself, and the alternating chain captures it: nothing is done without a condition that made it permissible. This is also why an EPC compiles almost as-is to event handlers — events are named states, functions are transitions, and the diagram hands you the transition table, not just a flowchart.

WarningWhen the model misrepresents

A fact is recorded as an act, or an act as a fact: “shipped” modelled as a step, “verify” modelled as an observation. Then the alternating chain collapses and the model no longer says what licensed what. Or the domain never draws the distinction: the business sees one move, not a fact that licenses an act. The alternation then forces an event and a function apart where the domain saw a single step, and the model asserts a difference the business never made.

TipThe faithfulness test

Is this element something we observed or something we did — and does every action have a state that licensed it?

  • If the distinction holds → EPC’s alternation is the domain’s own grammar.
  • If not → the chain has lost the knowledge, or the domain never drew the distinction.
  • If you can’t answer → the gap is the triggering condition, which is where domain knowledge is usually thinnest.

Case study: SLA / Escalation

🔥 Process models are different from design patterns: the artifact claims to describe the domain, so the gap question is not “should we use this?” but “does this model tell the truth?” Between the process (a knowledge claim) and the notation (modelling vocabulary) sits every silent misrepresentation.

Reading a process model? Ask “does the business actually work this way?” If you can’t answer, the model has recorded an assumption as knowledge. If you can, the model is the domain’s own shape — the notation adds nothing the process hadn’t already decided.

Dumas et al.: A process commitment with a time bound and an escalation path taken when it is missed. [8]

The domain knowledge gap it signals

The fact that the business has promised a time in which something will happen — and a defined response when it does not — is domain knowledge. “Claims are acknowledged within two days”; “if the approval is not given by Friday, it goes to the manager.” Deadlines, escalation, and delegation are that fact made executable: the promise, the clock, and the authority to escalate.

ImportantWhen the model is faithful

The domain states the promise, the clock, and who acts when it lapses. The model records the domain’s own obligation (“we owe this within X”) and its own escalation path (“then this role decides”). The timer looks technical; the bound itself is a domain decision — the promise’s clock.

WarningWhen the model misrepresents

A timeout is added for resilience — “give up after 30 seconds.” Even this is a domain decision: someone chose the bound. If the domain has not named the promise and the response, the timeout asserts a rule nobody decided. Or a real SLA is invisible: the model contains no clock for a promise the business makes to customers, so lateness is discovered by complaint. Both claims are about time: one asserts an unstated bound, the other hides a stated one. The escalations the business actually uses — four-eyes rules, delegation, standing authority — are left out of the model entirely.

TipThe faithfulness test

Has the business promised a time here, and named who acts when it passes?

  • If yes → the clock and the escalation are the domain’s own promise.
  • If no → the timeout still encodes a chosen bound; the gap is that the domain has not stated the promise. Calling it an SLA asserts an obligation nobody made.
  • If you can’t answer → the gap is the promise: nobody has said how long is acceptable or what happens if it lapses.
